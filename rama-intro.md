# Why Swami Rama’s Methods Deserve Attention

In 1970, at the Menninger Foundation in Topeka, Swami Rama worked with researchers Elmer and Alyce Green on voluntary control of functions then regarded as largely involuntary. The demonstrations, recorded under laboratory conditions and later described in Green & Green’s *Beyond Biofeedback* (1977), included:

- Differential blood-flow control in one hand, producing a temperature difference of roughly 11 °F between adjacent areas of the palm without movement.
- Induction of atrial flutter (heart rate rising to approximately 300 beats per minute) lasting about 17 seconds. During that interval the chambers did not fill effectively, the pulse was obliterated, and blood pressure dropped. It was not a flatline arrest; the record showed flutter that stopped effective pumping. Swami noted that “when you stop your heart this way it still flutters in there.”
- Voluntary production of alpha, theta, and (during a yoga-nidra demonstration) predominantly delta brain-wave patterns while remaining aware enough to accurately recall events in the room.

His stated purpose was not to claim magic, but to show that control of the mind can produce control of autonomic processes involved in psychosomatic illness.

These results matter for two practical reasons. First, they supply evidence that extreme degrees of autonomic regulation are trainable in a highly practiced individual. Second, the same tradition that produced the laboratory demonstrations also produced systematic methods for everyday use: regulating the four basic drives (food, sleep, sex, and self-preservation), observing impulses without immediate action, and deliberately practicing new responses so that old habit patterns lose their force.

The extreme feats are not the point for most people. The usable part is the sequence that precedes them: reduce the raw material that feeds unwanted patterns by regulating the basic drives, then observe and replace the responses that still appear. Those steps are laid out in the notes on regulating the four basic drives and on changing habit patterns. They can be started immediately, measured week by week, and adjusted with ordinary tools (consistent cues, reduced friction, simple logs) without any requirement to reproduce the Menninger demonstrations.

The laboratory work shows that the direction of training is not empty. The practical notes show how to walk in that direction with the resources you already have.
